Magnesium Spray vs Pills: Which Is Better?

Published October 26, 2026 · By Magnesium Daily Editorial Team

For correcting a magnesium deficiency, oral magnesium is the option with supporting evidence. The available evidence does not show magnesium spray meaningfully raising blood magnesium levels, and a 2017 review called transdermal magnesium scientifically unsupported. Spray is mostly a routine and comfort choice, and pills can upset the stomach.

What each one is actually for

Oral magnesium, in capsules, tablets, powders or medicines, is the form that has been studied for correcting low magnesium. A 2017 review in Nutrients noted that numerous studies demonstrate the effectiveness of oral supplementation and called transdermal magnesium a not yet proven form of application (Gröber et al., 2017).

Magnesium spray, sometimes sold as magnesium oil, is magnesium chloride dissolved in water and applied to the skin. People mostly use it on the legs, feet or shoulders as part of an evening routine. That is a routine and a matter of comfort, and the evidence does not let us call it a way of topping up your magnesium.

Absorption: the claim behind most sprays

The usual pitch is that skin bypasses the gut, so a spray absorbs better and avoids stomach upset. The first half of that has not held up. The available evidence does not show topical magnesium meaningfully raising blood magnesium levels. The skin is a barrier by design, and the human trials have been small and used unreliable measures. The same review concluded that promoting transdermal magnesium is scientifically unsupported, while stopping short of saying it has been disproven. Larger studies are still called for.

The stomach side of pills

The second half of the pitch has something real behind it. Magnesium taken by mouth in supplement or medicine form can cause diarrhea, sometimes with nausea and abdominal cramping (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements). People who find pills hard on the stomach are the ones most drawn to a spray.

The trade-off is plain. A spray may spare the stomach, but the evidence does not show it doing the job pills are used for. If low magnesium is the concern, that is a conversation with a doctor about which form to use, not a swap you can safely make on your own.

What about cramps and sleep?

Neither form has strong evidence for cramps. The 2020 Cochrane review concluded that magnesium supplementation is unlikely to provide clinically meaningful cramp prevention for older adults (Garrison et al., 2020). Who should be careful

Magnesium can interact with some prescription medications, and the kidneys limit how much of it the body holds on to, so anyone with reduced kidney function should talk to a doctor before using magnesium in any form. The same applies if you are pregnant or have a heart or kidney condition. Testing a small area of skin first is a sensible precaution with a spray.

Frequently asked questions

Magnesium Spray vs Pills: Which Is Better?
For deficiency, the evidence favors oral magnesium. A 2017 review found oral supplementation well documented and transdermal use not yet proven, and the available evidence does not show topical magnesium meaningfully raising blood magnesium levels. Spray is mostly chosen for routine and comfort, which is user experience, not evidence of an effect.

Is magnesium spray better absorbed than pills?
Nothing in the evidence shows that it is. The claim that skin bypasses the gut and absorbs better has not been supported by the human studies, which have been small and used unreliable measures.

Does magnesium spray avoid the stomach side effects of pills?
Taking magnesium by mouth in supplement or medicine form can cause diarrhea, sometimes with nausea and abdominal cramping, according to the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements. A spray avoids that, but the trade-off is that the evidence does not show it raising blood magnesium.

Can I use magnesium spray and pills together?
Questions about combining forms are best asked of a doctor or pharmacist, particularly if you take prescription medications or have reduced kidney function, since magnesium can interact with some medicines.

Which is better for muscle cramps, spray or pills?
Neither has good evidence. A 2020 Cochrane review concluded that magnesium supplementation is unlikely to provide clinically meaningful cramp prevention for older adults, and research on topical magnesium for cramps is limited.

Who should talk to a doctor before using either?
Anyone with reduced kidney function, anyone taking prescription medications, and anyone who is pregnant or has a heart or kidney condition. The kidneys limit how much magnesium the body holds on to, which is why kidney function matters.
